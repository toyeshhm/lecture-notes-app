Lecture Notes

Press record when the lecturer starts talking. The notes are filed in your vault before you get home.

A Mac app that records a lecture, transcribes it on this machine's Neural Engine, and writes structured study notes into the right course folder of an Obsidian vault you already keep.

Three things to know before you download it

It is not notarized.

There is no Apple Developer account behind this, so there is no certificate to sign it with and nothing to submit to Apple's notary service. It carries an ad-hoc signature, which proves the bundle has not been altered since it was built and proves nothing at all about who built it. macOS attaches a quarantine flag to anything a browser downloads, and on a quarantined app with no notarization Gatekeeper refuses the first double-click outright and offers you the Trash.

The way through, on macOS 15 and later: double-click the app, let macOS refuse, then open System Settings › Privacy & Security, scroll down to the Security section, and click Open Anyway beside the message naming the app. Confirm once and it launches from then on. Older instructions tell you to Control-click and choose Open. Sequoia removed that override for apps that are not notarized, so it no longer does anything. If vouching for a stranger's binary is not a trade you want to make, build it from source instead and the quarantine flag never gets attached in the first place.

It needs the claude CLI, logged in to a paid subscription.

Transcription is local and costs nothing. The notes are a different matter: they are written by a model, through the claude command line tool, spending whatever Claude subscription you already pay for. One detection pass at the start, a short pass every few minutes during the lecture, one full pass over everything when you stop. That comes out of your normal usage limit; it is not a separate pool and it is not unlimited. Without a login you get a recording and a transcript and no notes at all. This is the requirement people miss.

First launch downloads about 600 MB of models.

The CoreML speech models are fetched once, on first run. Do that at home the night before rather than in the ninety seconds before a lecture starts. There is a whole download screen for it, which exists so you find out early instead of finding out in a lecture theatre.

What you actually end up with

Not a transcript, and not a screen inside another app you now have to check. A markdown file in the vault you already keep, which Obsidian reads as an ordinary note. This is the real output for a lecture on binary search trees, trimmed in the middle where the notes run long.

The frontmatter is what Obsidian shows as properties, so the notes sort and filter by course and date without you tagging anything. The callout is there because the lecturer said it out loud; marking what was flagged as examinable is the one editorial judgement the app makes.

What it does

Audio goes to disk as it arrives. Not buffered in memory and written at the end. If the app crashes, or the battery goes, you lose the transcript and you still have the recording.

Transcription runs here. Parakeet TDT v3 through CoreML, on the Neural Engine. No audio ever leaves the machine. To be exact about what that does and does not mean: the recording stays put, but the text of the transcript is sent to Claude, because that is what the notes get written from.

Notes arrive during the lecture, not only after it. Every few minutes Claude writes an interim set from what has been said so far, so a lecture that ends badly, or a laptop that dies at minute fifty, still produced something worth reading.

When you stop, it starts over properly. The whole recording is transcribed again in a single pass, which is noticeably more accurate than the streaming text, and the note is written from that. The interim notes were the insurance policy; this is the real one.

It works out which course this was. Detection reads the transcript, matches it against the course folders already in your vault and against a roster if you keep one, and returns a course and a topic. When it is not confident it files under _Unsorted and says so in the note. It does not guess. A misfiled note quietly corrupts your revision material three months later, which is a worse outcome than an unfiled one sitting where you can see it.

A second copy, if you want one. Configure a mirror vault and every lecture is written into both, from the same source, in one pass. Nothing is ever synced from a copy.

The output is plain Obsidian markdown. YAML frontmatter with course, date, duration and status. Headings, lists, callouts. Anything the lecturer flagged as examinable comes back as an > [!important] callout, so the thing they said would be on the final is the thing you see first at 11pm. The library is read straight off the vault every time, with no index and no cache, so if you rename or move a note in Obsidian the app agrees with you on the next scan.

Where the notes go

You pick the vault and the two folder names. The course folder itself comes from detection, and is created if it is not there yet.

MyVault/
  00 - Courses/
    CS 314H/
      Lectures/
        2026-09-02 — Binary Search Tree Deletion.md
        2026-09-04 — Red-Black Trees.md
    M 340L/
      Lectures/
        2026-09-02 — Alternating Series Test.md
    _Unsorted/
      Lectures/
        2026-09-04 — Course Administration.md

That last one is what a lecture looks like when detection was not sure. It carries a warning at the top of the note and the guess in the frontmatter. Detection runs again at the end of the lecture, and if the answer changes the note moves itself rather than leaving a duplicate behind.

Getting it running

Download the zip from the release, unzip it, drag LectureNotes.app into /Applications, then read the first section on this page again before you double-click it, because it will not open. If you would rather not go through System Settings, strip the flag yourself:

xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine /Applications/LectureNotes.app

Either way you are the one vouching for the binary. Building it yourself avoids the question entirely, and takes about a minute:

git clone https://github.com/toyeshhm/lecture-notes-app.git
cd lecture-notes-app
brew install xcodegen
make app

And if you do not have the CLI yet:

npm install -g @anthropic-ai/claude-code
claude            # then type /login

What you need

An Apple silicon Mac
M1 or later. The speech models run on the Neural Engine through CoreML. There is no Intel build and no CPU fallback.
macOS 14 or later
Sonoma. On Sequoia and later, the Open Anyway route described above is the only one through the interface.
The claude CLI, logged in
A Pro or Max subscription is what makes this free at the margin. An ANTHROPIC_API_KEY works too, but it bills per token.
An Obsidian vault
Or any folder at all. The notes are plain markdown with YAML frontmatter. Obsidian is where they are pleasant to read, not where they have to live.
About 600 MB of disk
One time, on first launch, for the models.

Expect to grant microphone access more than once if you build from source. macOS keys that permission to a code signature, an ad-hoc signature is regenerated on every build, so a rebuild can produce something macOS considers a different app. It looks exactly like a bug and it is not one.

What it does not do

It is not sandboxed, and it is not on the App Store. It spawns the claude binary and writes into a vault that lives wherever you keep it, and neither of those is possible inside an App Sandbox container. That rules out the Mac App Store on its own, notarization aside.

It will spend your subscription. Three kinds of pass per lecture, and a long lecture is a lot of transcript. Settings has the knobs.

It will not guess a course. Covered above, and worth repeating, because the failure mode it avoids is the expensive one.

The notes are a study aid, not a record. They are generated from speech recognition output and they can misattribute or drop things. Check anything that matters against the lecture itself, and do not hand them in as your own work.

Check your institution's policy before you record a lecture. Plenty require explicit permission and some prohibit it outright, and consent rules differ by jurisdiction. That part is on you, not on the tool.
